I've sold paintings, houses and digital programs.

Three trades that on paper have nothing to do with each other. Every time I was convinced I had to start over. Every time I was wrong.

At the gallery, back in 2012, I understood something that never left me: nobody buys a painting for the painting. They buy how it will feel to look at it for the next twenty years. My job wasn't to describe the work. It was to figure out where in that person's life it would end up.

Then eight years in real estate, up to running an agency. That's where I learned the uncomfortable part: the real negotiation is almost never with the client. It's inside the client's home. The husband who wants it, the wife who's holding back, the father who's funding it, the brother-in-law who knows a surveyor.

I wasn't closing a person. I was holding a table together.

When I moved to digital — 45-minute calls, a screen, a stranger — I expected to be years behind.

The only thing I was behind on were the tools. CRM, funnels, metrics, the vocabulary. Stuff I caught up on in a few months.

The rest I already had, and no course had taught me. The cases had.

I'm writing this because the market today says the opposite. Eight weeks to "become a closer", as if the craft were a list of lines to memorise.

Lines you can learn in an afternoon.

What you can't learn in an afternoon is telling, in the first few minutes of a call, whether the person in front of you is deciding or postponing. That builds up case by case, and cases take years.

That's not bad news, especially for anyone coming from another industry who thinks they're starting at a disadvantage.

If you've spent years selling something physical, face to face with a real person, you're not behind. You've already done the long part.
